# Dossier: Manfred Fricke, MFB (Manfred Fricke Berlin), and the MFB Tanzbär Analog Drum Machine

*Research compiled July 2026. Each section is self-contained for RAG chunking. Confidence flags are given inline where the record is thin — MFB's history is sparsely documented and the company's own website (mfberlin.de) is no longer online, so some facts rest on archived press, forum threads, and the original user manual. This machine is the CENTREPIECE of Alex's studio.*

## 1. Manfred Fricke — The Man

Manfred Fricke (born 8 September 1950, died 17 June 2021) was a Berlin engineer and the founder of MFB — "Manfred Fricke Berlin," formally *Ingenieurbüro Manfred Fricke*, based at Neue Straße 13, 14163 Berlin (Zehlendorf). He is regarded as one of the pioneers of affordable electronic musical instruments and one of the earliest electronic instrument builders in Berlin, decades before the city became famous for boutique synth manufacturing.

**Background.** By his own account (Amazona.de 40th-anniversary interview, 2019), Fricke originally studied architecture, discovered he enjoyed electronics more, and completed a second degree in electrical engineering. He had almost no formal musical background — he played a little guitar, and it was the desire for an electronic drum accompaniment for his guitar playing that led him into rhythm machines. In the early-to-mid 1970s he built video games in West Berlin, including a colour-TV "screen tennis" game he believed may have predated Atari's home Pong in Germany, and later a ~100-IC arcade machine board.

**Design philosophy.** Fricke's method was radical cost-engineering: take an ambitious published circuit (his first drum machine was inspired by the 1976 Elektor magazine "IC Drumbox" project), redesign it to a fraction of the component count, house it in a cheap grey plastic project box (earning early MFB gear the affectionate nickname "Tupperware-Drumbox"), and sell it at a price any musician could afford. He told Amazona.de he deliberately never moved upmarket: high-end production required investment he wasn't willing to make, and "there was — and still is — a market for budget construction." Even his final project, the polyphonic MFB Synth Pro, was explicitly framed by his family as continuing his focus on "being as affordable as possible for the customer." The trade-off — idiosyncratic interfaces, small buttons, variable build quality, minimal documentation — became as much a part of the MFB identity as the sound.

**Death — verified date.** Manfred Fricke died on **17 June 2021 at 10:58 a.m.**, peacefully and surrounded by family, after fighting a tumour for four years. He was 70. The news broke publicly around 28–29 June 2021 via Andreas Schneider (Stromkult/SchneidersLaden), then CDM, Matrixsynth, Synthtopia/Synth Anatomy, Bonedo (German), KVR, ModWiggler and Gearspace memorial threads. He left behind his wife Gloria and son Jean Marcel. A private memorial was held 3 July 2021 at the village church in Berlin-Zehlendorf.

**Tributes.** CDM's Peter Kirn wrote that Fricke "was democratizing analog machines for music and pioneering Berlin electronic invention over 5 decades — years ahead of this city becoming known for such things." Andreas Schneider noted: "The Tanzbär, in particular, is considered by many to be their favorite drum machine of the last few years, especially in terms of its amazing sound." Community threads repeatedly cited the MFB-501 as the machine on which many German musicians' careers began.

**MFB after his death.** The family stated in June 2021 that "MFB will continue to exist," with son Jean Marcel Fricke managing the company alongside Manfred's previous partners, while warning that a transition phase was inevitable and that Manfred's "individual and unique workflow" made him irreplaceable. In practice, the company faded: no significant new products appeared after 2021 (the last releases under Manfred were the MFB-501 Pro drum machine and the MFB Synth Pro 8-voice analog polysynth). By September 2023 ModWiggler threads ("MFB coming back?") reported the official domain mfberlin.de was up for sale, and as of this research (July 2026) mfberlin.de is a parked domain-for-sale page. **Confidence: high that MFB is commercially dormant/inactive as of 2026 with no active production or web presence; no formal insolvency or closure announcement was found, and rumours persist that Jean Marcel Fricke may eventually revive the brand — treat any "MFB is back" claim as unverified.**

Sources: cdm.link (RIP Manfred Fricke), amazona.de 40-Jahre-MFB interview, synthanatomy.com, matrixsynth.com, synthandsoftware.com (MFB into the future without Manfred), ModWiggler memorial + "MFB coming back?" threads, mfberlin.de (now parked).

## 2. MFB Company History and Product Lineage

MFB (Manfred Fricke Berlin) operated from West Berlin from the mid-1970s until roughly 2021. The company's history splits into three eras.

**Era 1 — Budget drum machines (1976/1979–1986).** MFB's founding date is given variously as 1976 (CDM timeline, family statement — covering the video-game work) and 1979 (first music product). The **MFB-301/II** IC-Drumbox (1979, 175 DM) was a preset rhythm box that sold over 1,000 units in Germany within months. The **MFB-501** (c. 1980–81, ~380 DM) was the breakthrough: a preset drum machine so cheap that some German shops reportedly sold ten MFB-501s for every Roland TR-808. Fricke himself called the 501 his most important product. There was also the rare **Otto I** external rhythm programmer, the **MFB-601** digital sequencer (1981, ~480 DM — reputedly used for the trumpet-solo sequence on Alphaville's "Forever Young"), the **MFB-512** (early sample-based machine using an ingenious 1-bit sample scheme with analog VCF/VCA post-processing to fit eight instruments on one memory chip), an E-Drum module series (1984), an Apple II-based drum computer (a commercial flop Fricke recalled fondly), and the **MFB-201** MIDI-to-CV converter (1986). In the UK, early MFB gear was distributed by Syco Systems alongside Fairlight and Linn.

**Era 2 — Video (1986–2000).** Feeling he had run out of instrument ideas as digital sampling took over, Fricke left the music market and built industrial framegrabbers for Apple II, Commodore 64, Atari and IBM PC, then the **MFB-902** (1995), the first video mixer under 1,000 DM, mass-distributed in partnership with HAMA.

**Era 3 — The analog revival (2000–2021).** Fricke re-entered music by selling a Minimoog-inspired **MFB-SYNTH** prototype on eBay and the sample-based **Kult** drum machine. There followed the **Synth Lite** and Synth Lite 2 desktop monosynths, the "Zwerg" family (**Kraftzwerg**, Schlagzwerg, Megazwerg, Microzwerg, **Nanozwerg**), a Eurorack module range, step sequencers (**MFB-STEP 64**, Urzwerg), the "5x2" drum machine line (**MFB-502, 503, 522** — the 522 being an 808-flavoured hit, the 503 909-flavoured), the flagship **Dominion 1** keyboard synth (c. 2014), and the Tanz-family of drum machines: **Tanzbär (2013)**, **Tanzmaus and Tanzbär Lite (2016)**, **Tanzbär 2 (2018)**. Final products were the **MFB-301 Pro** (2019 anniversary reissue), **MFB-501 Pro** (2020, classic analog voices plus sample slots), and the **Synth Pro** polysynth (2020–21).

**Why MFB gear is cult-loved.** The consistent formula: genuinely analog sound with real punch and character, unusually deep feature sets for the price, and compact form factors — packaged with rough edges. Reviews and forums repeatedly cite stiff mini-pots, tiny buttons, cryptic Shift-layer interfaces, thin manuals, and patchy customer service. Sound On Sound's verdict on the Tanzbär crystallised the brand: "Despite its shortcomings, I can't think of an analogue drum machine that sounds better, period."

Sources: amazona.de interview, cdm.link, synthandsoftware.com, synthanatomy.com (MFB-501 Pro), Sound On Sound Tanzbär review.

## 3. MFB Tanzbär (Original, 2013) — Overview and Feature Set

The MFB Tanzbär ("dancing bear"), full name **MFB Tanzbär Analoger Drumcomputer**, released 2013 at €840 including VAT (distributed via SchneidersLaden, Berlin), is a fully analog drum machine combining circuitry evolved from the MFB-522 (808-style) and MFB-503 (909-style) with a deep pattern-based step sequencer. Dimensions approximately 333 × 167 × 60 mm, weight ~1 kg, metal frame with wooden end-cheeks, powered by an external 12V DC adapter with an on/off switch (use only the supplied adapter or one of identical specification — exact polarity/current rating not verified in available sources; check the unit's rear label). No display, no headphone jack.

Headline spec:
- **14 analog drum voices / 17 drum instruments** (toms double as congas): Bassdrum 1, Bassdrum 2, Snare, Rimshot, Cymbal, Open Hi-hat, Closed Hi-hat, Claves, Claps, Low/Mid/High Tom-or-Conga, Cowbell, Maracas. Up to 8 tweakable, per-step-storable parameters per voice.
- **Two simple digital synth voices** (lead and bass) driven by two note tracks, primarily intended as sources for the CV/Gate and MIDI note outputs.
- **Sequencer:** 144 patterns (3 sets × 3 banks × 16), 14 drum tracks + 2 note tracks, A/B pattern halves, per-track length and shuffle, per-step sound parameter locks (Elektron-style), accent, flam (16 types, per step), roll with record, bend (pitch glides), chain of up to 16 patterns, track mutes saved per pattern.
- **I/O:** main stereo out + 6 individual outs (stereo jacks carrying two voices each), two MIDI Ins, one MIDI Out, CV1/CV2/CV3, Gate1/Gate2, analog Sync and Start jacks (mini-jack, software-configurable in/out).

Tempo range is approximately 60–180 BPM, adjusted with Shift + Data knob (no numeric tempo display; the value only changes once the knob passes the stored position).

Sources: Sound On Sound (Paul Nagle, November 2013), MFB-Tanzbär User Manual (manuals.plus mirror), Resident Advisor tech review.

## 4. Tanzbär Voices and Per-Voice Parameters (from the official manual)

- **BD1 (Bassdrum 1):** Attack (transient level), Decay, Pitch (pitch-envelope time/intensity), Tune, Noise, Filter; Data knob = noise character; hidden "Sound" parameter = distortion level (16 values via Sound button + step keys). The deep, widely configurable kick with 909-like reach.
- **BD2 (Bassdrum 2):** Decay (up to steady tone), Tune, Tone (blend of two tone signals), Pitch. Simpler, smoother, more 808-like.
- **SD (Snare):** Tune, D-Tune (detune of tone 2), Snappy (noise level), S-Decay (noise decay), Tone (blend), Decay; Data = pitch-envelope intensity.
- **RS (Rimshot):** Pitch/Tune (CC 88), Data.
- **CY (Cymbal):** Decay, Tone (blend), Data = pitch/colour; Tune via CC.
- **OH / HH (Open/Closed Hi-hat):** each has Decay; Data = shared pitch/colour of both hats.
- **CL (Claves):** Tune, Decay.
- **CP (Claps):** Decay ("reverb" tail), Filter (colour), Attack (transient level), Data = number of attack transients, hidden Sound = 16 different attack-transient patterns (some staggered/delayed) — a signature clap, stereo at the output.
- **LTC / MTC / HTC (Low/Mid/High Tom-Conga):** Tune, Decay (up to drone); hidden Sound: Step 12 toggles tom↔conga, Step 13 toggles a noise component; Data = noise level (global for all three).
- **CB (Cowbell):** Data = 16 tunings; hidden Sound = decay.
- **MA (Maracas):** Data = decay.
- **Bass synth / CV3:** Data = filter cutoff (or CV3 value).

Note: bass drums, toms and congas deliberately reach very long decays (into drones) in the last few degrees of the Decay knob — useful with Bend for kick-into-bassline morphs, but a known cause of accidental droning. Individual instrument volume pots and master volume are analog and **cannot** be programmed, recorded into patterns, or controlled via MIDI.

Sources: official manual, Sound On Sound review.

## 5. Tanzbär Sequencer Capabilities

- **Modes:** Three record modes — **Manual** (no parameter recording; knobs always live), **Step** (per-step parameter locks; factory default), **Jam** (like Step, but all knobs live across the whole track while recording). Cycle with Shift + Step 15 (green = Manual, red = Step, orange = Jam), confirm with Select. Separately, the Rec/ManTrg button toggles: LED off = Play mode (step buttons mute instruments), green = Manual Trigger mode (step buttons play voices; also the mode required to use the Tanzbär as a MIDI drum expander), red = recording steps for the selected instrument.
- **Pattern memory:** 3 Sets (A = factory, B/C = user) × 3 banks × 16 patterns = 144. Select set with Shift + Set A, bank with Shift + Bank, then a step button. Pattern changes land on the next downbeat; the sequencer always finishes the current pattern before switching or stopping.
- **A/B halves:** every pattern has a B part; with 24/32-step scales A+B glue into one long pattern (always an even step count). When starting from an empty pattern, switching to the other half auto-copies your work. Auto A/B toggle: Shift + Step 3. Copy A→B: press A/B in Record mode.
- **Per-track length (polymeter):** 1–16 steps per track (Rec/ManTrg + track, Shift + Step Length, choose 1–16, Select), freely adjustable during playback — the Tanzbär's signature polyrhythm feature.
- **Scale and Measure (global, set in Play mode):** Scale selects resolution (32nd, 16th-triplet, 16th, 8th-triplet → 32/24/16/12 steps); Measure sets pattern step count for odd meters (e.g., scale 16 + measure 12 = 3/4).
- **Shuffle:** 16 intensities; per-track in Record mode (Rec/ManTrg + track, Shuffle, Step 1–16), global in Play mode (global overrides track settings).
- **Accent:** three levels (off / green / red) per step via Acc/Bnd; also affects Roll dynamics in Play mode. The bass (CV2) track accepts accents too.
- **Bend:** Shift + Acc/Bnd, intensity via Data knob; gradual pitch bends between steps on BD1, BD2, SD, LTC, MTC, HTC.
- **Flam:** 16 flam timings, programmable per step (Roll/Flam + step); not on Clap, CV1, CV2/3. (The panel misprints it as "Flames.")
- **Roll:** live note-repeat on one or more voices; resolution via Roll/Flam + Steps 1–4; **Roll Record** (Shift + Step 10) writes rolls into the pattern and doubles as the only real-time MIDI note-record path (basic, with audible latency).
- **Knob record:** live parameter tweaks captured over one bar; enable via Shift + Step 11 — counter-intuitively you must *leave* record mode first. Recorded tweaks are lost unless the pattern is stored.
- **Chain:** hold Chain + step buttons to chain up to 16 patterns live. The **Fill** button is *not* a drum fill — it's part of the chain/loop-structure system, and pressing it accidentally after editing can switch patterns and silently discard unstored edits.
- **Note tracks CV1 and CV2/3:** step-entered (Rec/ManTrg + CV button; steps 1–13 = notes C–c, 14–16 = octave, A/B = rest, Select ties steps for longer notes), 3-octave range, with slides/accents; CV3 is a per-step auxiliary CV lane (typically external filter cutoff) entered with the Data knob.
- **Storing:** Shift + St Patt, pick a location (lit = occupied), Select to confirm. Clear pattern: Shift + Cl Patt. **There is no undo for store or clear.** Kits as such do not exist — sound settings live inside patterns/steps, which is why "saving a kit" advice from other machines doesn't map onto the Tanzbär.

Sources: official manual, Sound On Sound review.

## 6. Tanzbär Setup and Configuration Reference (MIDI, Sync, Outputs, CV/Gate)

**MIDI channels (fixed):** CV1 (lead) track = **channel 1**; CV2 (bass) track = **channel 2**; all 14 drum tracks send/receive notes on **channel 3** (drum note/channel assignments are re-learnable via MIDI Trigger Learn, Setup Step 1). **Control Change (CC) data is received only at MIDI Input 1 and only on channel 10** — a classic gotcha documented both in the manual and in Sound On Sound's review. The panel knobs do **not** transmit CCs. MIDI In 1 is for keyboards/controllers; MIDI In 2 is intended for MIDI clock (both inputs accept notes, so choose deliberately). MIDI clock is always sent and received; uniquely, the Tanzbär stops sending MIDI clock when stopped, which can disable clock-synced delay pedals.

**Default drum note map (channel 3, learnable):** BD1 = 36, BD2 = 37, SD = 38, RS = 39, CY = 40, OH = 41, HH = 42, CL = 43, CP = 44, LTC = 45, MTC = 46, HTC = 47, CB = 48, MA = 49. CV1 and CV2/3 tracks respond to notes 36–72. (Cross-checked between the official manual appendix and the Squarp Pyramid community definition files.)

**MIDI CC map (verified against the official manual appendix; receive-only, channel 10, MIDI In 1):**
BD1: Attack 2, Tune 3, Noise 4, Filter 5, Distortion 6, Decay 64, Pitch 65, Trigger 66 · BD2: Decay 8, Tune 9, Tone 10 · SD: Tune 11, D-Tune 12, Snappy 13, Tone 14, Snappy-Decay 67, Tone-Decay 68, Pitch 69 · RS: Tune 88 · CY: Tone 15, Decay 70, Tune 71 · OH: Decay 72 · HH: Tune 73, Decay 74 · CL: Tune 16, Decay 17 · CP: Filter 18, Decay 75, Attack 76, Trigger 77 · HTC: Tune 19, Decay 20, Noise on/off 78, Tom/Conga 79 · MTC: Tune 21, Decay 22, Noise on/off 80, Tom/Conga 81 · LTC: Tune 23, Decay 24, Noise on/off 82, Tom/Conga 83 · Tom Noise (all) 84 · CB: Tune 85, Decay 86 · MA: Decay 87 · Set Select: CC 0 (value 0–2) · **Track Delay (micro-shift per track, MIDI-only feature): CC 89 (CV1), 90 (CV2/3), 91 (BD1), 92 (BD2), 93 (SD), 94 (RS), 95 (CY), 96 (OH), 97 (HH), 98 (CL), 99 (CP), 100 (LTC), 101 (MTC), 102 (HTC), 103 (CB), 104 (MA).** Track delay only shifts tracks *later*; to make one track feel early, delay all the others.

**Setup menu (Shift + Step 16, choose function with Steps 1–10, values toggle off/red/green, confirm with Select):**
1 MIDI Trigger Learn · 2 Synth tuning (440 Hz reference) · 3 Lead synth on/off · 4 Bass synth on/off (disable either when using CV/Gate to drive external synths) · 5 Sync clock divider (green = off, 24 PPQN DIN-sync standard; red = divider follows Scale value) · 6 Mute group (BD1 mutes BD2, or vice versa, or off) · 7 Clear current pattern bank (press twice; no undo) · 8 Global MIDI note send on/off · 9 Start/Stop socket mode: impulse (red) vs constant level (green) · 10 Factory reset (global settings only; user patterns preserved; factory patterns were downloadable from the MFB website — now offline, so keep SysEx backups).

**Analog sync:** Sync and Start mini-jacks are configurable as inputs or outputs (Shift + Sync/Step 13); when set as input the Tanzbär is slaved and its Play button is disabled. Gate, clock and start signals are **3V** — deliberately vintage-friendly in format but too low for some vintage machines; check trigger thresholds. Gate 1/Gate 2 can be inverted independently (Shift + Gate/Step 14). CV outputs are 1V/octave; CV1 = lead pitch, CV2 = bass pitch, CV3 = auxiliary/filter CV.

**Individual outputs and jack normalling:** one main stereo out plus six individual outs on stereo (TRS) 1/4" jacks, each carrying **two voices** (except Clap, which is genuinely stereo): BD Out (L = BD1, R = BD2), SD/RS Out, HH/CY Out (L = hats, R = cymbal), CP/Clap Out, TO/CO Out (three toms/congas panned across the field), CB/CL Out (L = clave, R = cowbell). **Inserting a plug into an individual out removes those voices from the main mix** — both voices on that jack. You need insert/Y-cables (TRS to dual TS) to split pairs to separate mixer channels; this is by far the most complained-about physical quirk. There are no pan controls and no MIDI control of levels or panning.

Sources: official manual incl. MIDI appendix, Squarp community note/CC map, Sound On Sound, ManualsLib.

## 7. Tanzbär Known Quirks and Common Confusions (Why It's "Harder to Set Up")

1. **Everything hides behind Shift.** With no display, nearly every function is a Shift-combo mapped to a step button, distinguished only by red/green/orange LED states. ModWiggler users describe the Tanz-family workflow as "utterly baffling" until memorised; Sound On Sound recommended memorising the Shift map and LED colours before anything else. The step buttons' printed sub-labels (Setup = 16, Sync = 13, Gate = 14, Man/Step = 15, Dump = 9, Roll Rec = 10, Knob Rec = 11) are the real "menu system."
2. **Three overlapping mode systems.** Play vs Manual Trigger vs Record (the Rec/ManTrg LED: off/green/red) is independent of Manual/Step/Jam record modes — conflating the two is the most common source of "why isn't it responding" confusion.
3. **CCs silently ignored.** CCs must arrive on MIDI Input 1, channel 10 — not channel 3 where the drums listen for notes. Users controlling the Tanzbär from a DAW on channel 3 conclude MIDI CC "doesn't work."
4. **Nothing is saved automatically.** Knob-record tweaks, step edits and mutes vanish unless the pattern is explicitly stored (Shift + St Patt); Fill/Chain or pattern changes can discard unstored edits with no undo. Power-up starts with no pattern loaded — by design.
5. **Individual outs pull voices from the mix** in pairs — patching one voice out silently "loses" its partner from the main outs.
6. **Stop happens at pattern end, not immediately**; sync starts/stops on pattern boundaries. Helpful in multi-machine rigs, confusing on first contact.
7. **Drone traps:** the last few degrees of BD/tom Decay knobs create sustained tones.
8. **Knob-record requires leaving record mode first** — the single most cited "unintuitive" behaviour in reviews.
9. **Hardware:** stiff/rough mini volume pots (widely reported), tiny buttons, no numeric tempo, no headphone out. 3V gate/clock may not fire older vintage gear.
10. **Firmware cannot be updated by the user via MIDI.** Pattern **SysEx dumps** (Shift + Dump/Step 9; MIDI-OX on Windows, SysEx Librarian on Mac recommended) are for backing up pattern banks only, and *receiving* a dump overwrites the current bank without warning.

Sources: Sound On Sound, ModWiggler Tanzbär threads, Gearspace MFB Tanzbär thread, official manual.

## 8. Tanzbär Firmware History and Serviceability After Fricke's Death

**Firmware.** The original Tanzbär has no user-friendly update path: Sound On Sound documented that installing an OS update on early units required *soldering a USB cable directly to the circuit board* — there is no SysEx/.MID firmware update on the mk1 (SysEx is patterns only). Forum reports indicate this soldered-header situation applied to the first production batch, and that owners could send units back to MFB for updates. A firmware v1.03 is referenced in secondhand listings ("MFB Tanzbär V. 1.03"), but **no official changelog or version history for the mk1 could be found — confidence low; do not assume specific versions/fixes.** (Note the contrast: the later MFB Tanzmaus, Tanzbär Lite and Tanzbär 2 all update via a MIDI SysEx bootloader — online update instructions mentioning SysEx almost always refer to those models, not the original Tanzbär.)

**Serviceability (2026).** With MFB dormant and its website offline, there is no factory service. The good news, per ModWiggler spare-parts threads: MFB designs use predominantly discrete, off-the-shelf components, making them tractable for any competent synth tech — though component substitutions between production runs and mediocre factory soldering are reported. Known third-party options: **Acme Revival** (US) lists service for MFB machines; **Réparation Pro Audio Repair / audiotechnik.ca** (Canada) has been recommended for Tanzbär work on Gearspace; in Europe, **SchneidersLaden (Berlin)** historically handled MFB distribution and remains the best first contact for pointers. The stiff mini-pots are generic parts. **Practical advice: back up pattern banks via SysEx now, keep the original 12V PSU (or note its exact spec before replacing), and treat the unit as irreplaceable — factory pattern downloads and manuals now exist only via mirrors (ManualsLib, manuals.plus, GitHub manual archives) and archive.org captures of mfberlin.de.**

Sources: Sound On Sound, ModWiggler mk1 spare-parts thread, Gearspace (Canadian tech for Tanzbär repair), acmerevival.com, maquinitas-manuals on GitHub.

## 9. Model Comparisons — Which Online Advice Applies to the Original Tanzbär

- **MFB Tanzbär (2013, the original / "mk1"):** fully analog, 14 drum voices/17 instruments + 2 CV/MIDI note tracks, no display, no samples, 144 patterns, CC receive on ch. 10, firmware not user-updatable. Advice mentioning sample slots, an LCD, or SysEx firmware updates is **not** about this machine.
- **MFB Tanzmaus (2016, ~€450):** compact sibling voiced closer to the Roland TR-909 side; five analog voices plus **two sample channels** (16 samples each, user-loadable via SysEx); firmware updates via MIDI. Different voice architecture — parameter advice doesn't transfer.
- **MFB Tanzbär Lite (2016, ~€500):** nine analog voices in a Tanzmaus-style compact metal case with pads; essentially a stripped 808-flavoured subset of the Tanzbär engine, no CV note tracks, MIDI-updatable firmware. Sequencer workflow is similar in spirit, so *workflow* threads about the Lite partially apply; connection/MIDI specifics do not.
- **MFB Tanzbär 2 (2018, ~€950):** the successor, but a **hybrid**: 8 analog + 8 digital instruments — analog side includes two kicks, two snares, three tom/conga voices and a bass synth with an SSI filter; digital side has three sample-based voices plus an analog multimode filter; adds an **LCD display**, improved sequencer, and MIDI SysEx bootloader firmware updates (latest known Tanzbär 2 OS: 1.03). It is *not* a drop-in replacement for the all-analog original, and many players prefer the mk1's pure-analog voice set.

Sources: MusicRadar Tanzmaus/Tanzbär Lite review, CDM Tanzbär 2, ManualsLib Tanzbär 2 manual, Sonicstate review, SchneidersLaden.

## 10. Tanzbär Sound Character and Reception

The Tanzbär's circuit lineage runs directly through MFB's own MFB-522 (TR-808-flavoured) and MFB-503 (TR-909-flavoured): Roland-inspired analog voices with vastly expanded synthesis control and MFB's own colouration. Sound On Sound's Paul Nagle summarised it as "generally comparable to Roland's TR808, but with a much-expanded synthesis," with BD1 and the snare reaching 909-like territory "while keeping a definite flavour of their own," and concluded: "I can't think of an analogue drum machine that sounds better, period." Resident Advisor called it "an absolute cracker" that "sounds fantastic" while warning of the button-command learning curve. Attack Magazine included it among the ten best drum machines released since 2010. The per-step parameter-lock sequencing (an analog answer to Elektron), per-track polymeter and the 16-transient stereo clap are the most-praised distinctives. **Named artist/track documentation is thin — confidence low; avoid asserting specific artist usage.**

Sources: Sound On Sound, Resident Advisor, Attack Magazine ten-best list, CDM.

## 11. Tanzbär Market, Production, and Collectability

The original MFB Tanzbär launched in 2013 at €840 (Germany) / ~$1,000–1,200 street. Production ran until roughly the Tanzbär 2's 2018 arrival (**exact discontinuation date undocumented — confidence medium**). All MFB production ceased around 2021 with Manfred Fricke's death and the company's subsequent dormancy, ending any prospect of new supply for any Tanz-family machine.

Secondhand market as of ~2025–2026: original Tanzbär units appear infrequently; observed asking prices cluster around **$900–1,100 USD / €800–1,000** for clean examples, i.e., holding at or above original retail — unusual for a 2013 product and a direct consequence of the closed supply. Community sentiment after Fricke's death reinforces the keep-don't-flip pattern. **Confidence: medium — sample size of public sales is small; treat the range as indicative, not a valuation.** Working units with documented firmware/service history, the original PSU, and pattern backups command the top of the range.

Sources: Reverb listings, soundgas.com, retrogearshop.com, equipboard.com, amazona.de interview.
